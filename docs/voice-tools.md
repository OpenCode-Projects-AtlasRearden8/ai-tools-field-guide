# Voice tools: the ears and the mouth

*Voice tools* are the AI features that **listen** (turn spoken audio into text — called *transcription* or *speech-to-text*) and **speak** (turn text into audio — called *text-to-speech* or *voices*). They're the quiet workhorses of this whole field: less futuristic than chat assistants, more minutes saved per week.

## What it actually does

The listening side is trained on enormous amounts of speech, so it turns your rambling into clean text — punctuation and all. The speaking side reads text aloud in surprisingly human voices. "Translate" in this world doesn't mean languages only; it means converting between *your voice* and *the page*.

## What people use it for

- **Dictation instead of typing:** voice-note a journal entry, an email draft, or a patient-handover note while making coffee, then paste the text somewhere.
- **Meeting and lecture notes:** record the meeting, get a clean transcript, then ask a chat assistant to summarize it.
- **Reading with your ears:** listen to that PDF on your commute. Your eyes are tired; this is legal.
- **A sounding voice for drafts:** have your essay read aloud — errors your eyes slide past become obvious when spoken.

## Try it now (2 minutes, free, on your phone)

Your phone already has this built in:

1. **On iPhone:** open Messages or Notes, tap the microphone on the keyboard, and speak a sentence with natural punctuation out loud: "Hi Maya, comma, running ten minutes late, period."
2. **On Android:** the Gboard keyboard has the same microphone.
3. Notice it wrote your punctuation *as punctuation*. That's dictation — the lowest-friction writing assistant ever made.

Then try the fancier version (3 minutes, free tier):

4. Go to **Otter.ai** or **Fireflies.ai** (both have free tiers) and, instead of a real meeting, hit record and talk for one minute about your day.
5. Stop and read the transcript. Fast, imperfect, and about 95% right.
6. If you have a chat assistant handy, paste the transcript in and ask:

   > Summarize this into three short bullets and one action item.

7. That two-step — *record, then summarize* — is the single most reused AI workflow of the next decade. You just did it solo.

## Plain-English terms you'll see

- **Transcript:** your spoken words as text. The raw output.
- **Text-to-speech (TTS):** words as voice. Podcast mode.
- **Live translation:** one person speaks, the other hears their language. Works "well enough" for most travel frames.

## The three honest limits

- **Background noise and accents still cost accuracy.** Use the transcript's built-in editor for names and jargon — the tool fixes easy words and stumbles on the rare ones.
- **Battery and Wi-Fi.** Live transcription quietly drinks both.
- **Don't record conversations without telling people.** It's rude at best and in many places it's the law. Ask first; it takes four seconds and removes all the drama.

## Where beginners bash their knees on the coffee table

- **Transcribing a whole meeting, then never using it.** The transcript isn't the outcome — the *summary with action items* is. Always take step 6.
- **Dictating formal writing as final copy.** Your spoken words are a draft. Speak it, paste it, then *edit it tighter*. Voice runs longer than fingers, and the tidy-up is where quality lives.
- **Expect accents to work perfectly on day one.** Accuracy improves as the tool adapts to you, and speaking a little slower and closer to the mic helps more than any setting.

> Mantra for the week: *record, summarize, act.*
