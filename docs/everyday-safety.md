# Everyday safety: privacy, limits, and gray hair

This is the shortest lesson in the book and possibly the most valuable. Nothing here is scary, and nothing here requires you to become "a security expert." It's about three habits that cover 95% of the ways an AI tool can hurt your day.

## Habit 1: Treat it like a polite stranger

A chat assistant is a well-meaning person you met at a coffee shop: helpful, quick to offer ideas, and *not somebody you hand your vital documents to*.

Concretely:

- **Type it, don't paste it, when it's sensitive.** Don't paste in your full SSN, your patient's chart, or that page of the contract your lawyer hasn't looked at yet. Type a description instead: "a letter from my landlord about repairs, mentioning mold" — not the letter.
- **Pause at anything about someone else.** Their name, face, address, or health information isn't yours to enter. (Nurses and teachers: this is the big one for you. De-identify first — "a patient with these symptoms…" — then ask.)
- **Work emails and company secrets** stay out of consumer AI tools for the same reason you don't post them on billboards. Where clerical officer #14 hangs out is not for you to decide.

## Habit 2: Remember it guesses — check what matters

You already know how an AI misstates things. That's fine when the stakes are "this paragraph is slightly off."

It's *not* fine when someone relies on your output. The one-line rule:

> **Low stakes: trust and move fast. High stakes: verify with a real source.**

What "verify" means, sized right:

- ✅ Re-read what the official source says (TSA, your state board of nursing, the IRS, the bank).
- ✅ Ask a human: your pharmacist, your accountant, your professor. You'd ask them anyway; now you ask a *better question*.
- ⚠️ Asking the *same AI tool* "are you sure?" is not a fact check. It just gets pleasantly confident again.

## Habit 3: Keep your money and your passwords out of this

Three small things, then we're done:

1. **Never give an AI tool a password, a verification code, or a full credit-card number.** No legitimate tool needs these. Ever. If you're asked, it's not you talking to a tool — it's a scammer using a tool to *sound like* one.
2. **Be suspicious of "improved" links in your email.** "Your account, sign in here" phishing now sounds better than ever because a computer wrote it. You already know the difference: real sites are the ones you navigate to yourself.
3. **The fix for creepy feelings is the same as always:** turn off chat history in settings if you like, delete old chats (most apps make this a click), and pick tools from known names. You don't need a security seminar.

## The one-page version for beginners

> My data: takes care of itself if I just don't hand over sensitive things.
> Its data: it's guessing; I check the important stuff.
> Slippage: real sites I navigate to myself, real passwords never shared.

## Where beginners bash their knees on the coffee table

- **Pasting a real legal/medical document, then blinking.** The rule ("describe, don't paste") covers this; the habit gets muscle memory around week three.
- **Copying a tool's confident answer about someone's health or money.** This is the *one* advance-notice rule of this whole book worth getting a tattoo about: **for anything a license, a bank, or a life depends on, someone with that license or that bank account looks at it before it becomes official.**
- **Scaring yourself out.** The bottom Honesty regret is spending years avoiding useful tools because someone once mentioned "privacy" darkly. You now know 95% of the story — it's a coffee shop, not a carnival of doom.

> Mantra for the week: *polite stranger, honest stranger.*
