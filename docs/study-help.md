# Study help: learn anything, faster

This is the lesson where AI tools stop being convenient and start being *transformational*, especially if you're a student, a career-changer, a nurse precepting a new grad, or an adult returning to any kind of learning after a long gap. The tools believes you learn by *doing*, and they can now generate practice faster than any textbook you own.

## What it actually does

Same guessing machine. But for learning, that property — "it guesses, quickly, and repeats without tiring" — is exactly what practice and feedback need. The most powerful study trick didn't come from AI at all; it's the *Feynman technique*: explain a thing simply to somebody. It's powerful because explaining is a conversation.

AI is the first tool in history that can play "somebody" twenty times in a row at 3am without getting bored. There's the magic. Not "it writes my essay" — **it will sit and talk about this topic as long as you want, and it will correct you patiently.**

## Try it now (5 minutes, free — pick any topic)

Any chat assistant works. Perplexity/Discussion.AI and Quizlet's free tier also work, but skip them for now; keep it to one tool until chat feels easy.

1. Pick anything you've *almost* mastered. We'll use "photosynthesis" — swap in your own topic: blood pressure regulation, MLA citations, whatever.
2. Ask:

   > I'm going to explain photosynthesis briefly. Then tell me if I got it right, in plain English. Here goes: plants take in sunlight, and mix it with water and carbon dioxide, and that makes sugar and oxygen.

3. It'll say: got it right overall, and then gently note what's missing (the energy *storage* part — that sugar is the "battery"). You just learned the miss in 20 seconds instead of at exam time.
4. Now the pro move — ask it to **hit back**:

   > Now ask me 5 questions about photosynthesis, one at a time. Make #2 and #5 hard. Don't reveal answers until I try.

5. Answer honestly out loud, making mistakes on purpose. Getting things wrong in a low-stakes chat is the *actual* mechanism of studying. It's uncomfortable on purpose.
6. And the classic:

   > Explain the light reactions like I'm smart but exhausted and hungry. Then explain the same thing in technical terms.

Why this works: your brain stores memory in one channel and *understanding* in another. Getting both is how teachers learn things. AI does both on demand.

## Other study moves worth stealing

- **Lecture → flashcards:** paste your professor's class notes into a chat assistant with: "Turn these into 12 flashcards. Question on the front, answer on the back, one concept per card." Then paste them into the free tier of any flashcard app.
- **The syllabus spread:** "Help me build a 3-week study calendar for midterms that respects that I work Saturdays" — it will plan around a real life, not a schedule-template.
- **Rubric self-check:** "Here's my essay and my teacher's rubric. Score it honestly against each row. Be strict." (The "be strict" is the magic of that prompt.)
- **Definitions you can hold:** "Explain 'statistically significant' with a metaphor about one of the following: bowling, recipes, or a hospital shift."

## The rule about learning and cheating

Simple, and worth saying kindly:

> Tools that *quiz* you deepen learning. Tools that *answer* for you when the task was to think can hollow it out.

The tool doesn't know which one it's doing from moment to moment — you're the steering wheel. If partway through a session you catch yourself asking for *the answer* instead of *the explanation*, you've got a decision to make: take the answer and lean on it (short-term easy, mid-term hollowing), or take the explanation and let your own brain do the final assembly. Do the second one, most of the time.

And to be completely plain: real schools' rules (and real state boards, if you carry a license) get the final word on what's allowed in graded and licensed work. Ask early, in writing, and you'll never have a bad day over this.

> Mantra for the week: *explanations for me, my head for the answers.*
