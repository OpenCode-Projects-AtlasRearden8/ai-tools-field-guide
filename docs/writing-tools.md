# Writing tools: help when the words won't come

This lesson is about the part of an AI tool that almost everyone uses and nobody calls by name: **getting words onto the page faster**. Whether you open ChatGPT, Gemini, Notion, Grammarly, or Google Docs with "Help me write," you are using a writing tool. The strategy below works in all of them.

## What it actually does

Same guessing machine as always — but aimed at *prose*. It has studied so many emails, essays, cover letters, and lab reports that it can imitate the shape of them on request. That's both the magic (a blank page turned into a draft in ten seconds) and the risk (its draft is *the average of everything*, which reads as smooth, calm, and a little bit like nobody in particular wrote it).

So the modern writing workflow is not "let AI write it." It's:

> **You provide the substance. It provides the momentum.**

## Try it now (4 minutes, free in any chat assistant)

This exercise builds a cover letter for a fictional nurse named Sam — you'll adapt the move for any writing job.

1. Dump the raw ingredients first (this is the real work; AI can't do it for you):

   > I need a 150-word cover letter. I'm Sam, an ER nurse with 6 years in a county hospital. I'm moving toward a patient-education role. Strengths: crowd control under pressure, plain-English teaching, new-grad mentorship. Target: a community health center.

2. Ask for a draft:

   > Using only those details, write the letter. Don't invent accomplishments.

   Notice the guardrail — *"don't invent"* — because the tool otherwise pads with happy fiction.

3. Now the step everyone skips: **make it sound like a person.**

   > Good. Now rewrite it to sound like a human. Shorter sentences. One concrete example. No words like "helped facilitate" or "striven." Right now it reads like a robot wearing Sam's name tag.

4. Read both versions out loud. The second one has a pulse. The difference is one extra question: *"what's wrong with the first one?"*

5. Optional final polish, if you have a tool that checks grammar (Grammarly free tier or the browser spellcheck do fine):

   > Fix grammar and punctuation only. Don't change my wording.

## The four jobs of a writing AI

Learn these four verbs and you'll never be stuck at the blank-page stage again:

- **Draft** — raw material from your facts.
- **Rewrite** — same meaning, different tone, length, or audience.
- **Critique** — "What's weak about this?" is the most-underrated prompt in existence.
- **Tighten** — "Cut this by a third without losing the point" saves more careers than any tip in this book.

## The unlearning people actually need

Most of us were taught to write like we're getting graded: big words, long sentences, build-up before the point. AI tools imitate whatever you model — feed them cautious corporate mush and they'll launder it back to you. Feed them voice notes and fragments and "I think it's mainly the night shifts" and they'll shape it without sanding off the life.

The tool can't tell you what you think. That part is still yours, always.

## Where beginners bash their knees on the coffee table

- **Publishing the first draft.** First drafts from a machine are *vague and slightly off-brand*. The second ask ("make it sound human") is where your writing shows up.
- **Letting it invent facts.** Anything it "adds" (an award, a statistic, a credential) needs your 10-second fact check. For anything a stranger would rely on, this step is not optional.
- **Using it for schoolwork where the rules say no.** Which settings allow what *does* change; the reliable shortcut is *use it to study and revise, not to submit*. Check the syllabus; it's faster than navigating a disciplinary process.
- **"Make it not sound like AI."** There's a real version of this ask: use a thesaurus thoughtfully, cut $3 words, and add one true detail only you know. That's a whole lesson in itself — you now have it.

> Mantra for the week: *it drafts, I decide.*

> Fun fact to normalize things: this entire book was drafted, revised, fact-checked, argued with, and polished by a human who used all four verbs above — heavily. Tools are the pen, not the hand.
