# Image tools: making pictures by describing them

An *image tool* turns a written description into a picture. "A calico cat in a library, painted in watercolor" becomes — well, a calico cat in a library, painted in watercolor. These tools are also excellent *editors*: they can change the style of a photo, remove a distracting lamp from the background of your eBay listing, or extend a cropped photo's edges.

## What it actually does

The tool learned by studying millions of picture-and-caption pairs. When you give it a caption, it paints its *guess* at what that scene looks like. This is why it can delight you (a perfectly composed birthday e-card in ten seconds) and why it can also mangle things (fingers, text inside the image, faces you care about). Guesses are great at moods and terrible at signatures.

## What people use it for

- One-off graphics: party invites, flyers, thank-you cards
- Illustrations: a blog header, a friendly character for a project
- Practical cleanup: brighten, sharpen, denoise, or erase things in your own photos
- Brainstorming visuals: "show me three logo ideas for a bakery called Warm Oven"
- Fun: pictures of your pets as knights. No notes.

## Try it now (2 minutes, free)

**DALL·E** inside ChatGPT (chat.openai.com), **Gemini's image maker** (gemini.google.com), and the free tier of **Adobe Express** are three no-cost ways in. Try this:

1. In a chat assistant, ask:

   > Draw a cozy reading nook for someone who loves rainy days and black tea.

2. Now make it matter more:

   > Same cozy reading nook, but the color palette is warm orange and forest green, and a sleeping grey cat is on the windowsill.

3. Notice the difference: the second prompt is a *brief*, not a wish. Details like palette and mood are what you control.

4. Try text inside an image:

   > Draw a simple brown sign that says "Warm Oven Bakery" with a bread loaf icon.

5. Look closely. Words inside images are the most common failure — if it garbles letters, that's normal, and the fix is a simpler phrase (or fewer words) in the sign.

## How to talk to an image tool

Prompt like you're briefing a good illustrator:

- **Subject:** what's in the picture (one or two nouns is plenty)
- **Style:** watercolor, comic, photograph, 3D render, crayon
- **Mood/palette:** cozy, stark; warm oranges, monochrome blue
- **Setting:** where the subject is, and the light

Short and specific beats long and vague. "A watercolor of a lighthouse at dusk, warm orange sky" texts better than a paragraph of adjectives.

## The four honest limits

- **Text in images will misspell.** Plan on it; fix it over the garbled letters with a normal editor if you need perfect words.
- **Hands and tiny objects glitch.** Regenerate numbers lottery; two or three rolls usually wins.
- **Don't fake people.** Generating a photorealistic person to imply a real person said or did something is where you cross a line. Stay on the fun side of it.
- **Nobody knows the copyright deep-lore.** House rules are still settling worldwide. For everyday personal and small-business use you're fine; don't sell "AI art" of trademarks or living artists' styles.

## Where beginners bash their knees on the coffee table

- **Expecting one perfect roll.** Three or four variations is normal, not failure.
- **Ignoring an editing tab.** Upload a *photo you took* and ask it to "remove the background" or "make this brighter" — most tools also do that, and it's the more useful half.
- **Over-coaching.** Amateur prompts add thirteen adjectives hoping something lands. Put the strongest few on the page and let the guesser breathe.

> Mantra for the week: *brief it like a person, roll the dice, keep the ones you love.*
